Four Gaps and One Chain: Where Thailand’s Smart-City and Digital-Government Promotion Stops Short of Action

* Correspondence: non.ar@depa.or.th · Data and code: sciti.nonarkara.org/research
Research question. Is the stalling of smart-city promotion and digital government in Thailand over the years consistent with (H1) a lack of inter-agency coordination, (H2) a lack of robust technology procurement, (H3) a lack of data-driven responsiveness, and (H4) the lack of an end-to-end monitoring-to-push system, such as rain to drainage or haze to shelter?

Abstract

Thailand has promoted smart cities and digital government for a decade, and its international e-government rank has improved, yet the chain that matters, a measurement that becomes a message that moves a person, is still open. We treat four common explanations as hypotheses and ask what national evidence can and cannot say about each. We combine (i) the 118-city SCITI registry across 77 provinces and depa’s funnel from 227 proposals to 37 declared smart cities, (ii) a single-coder hand coding of the 76 headline projects named in 21 endorsed city records, by the link of the monitoring-to-push chain their titles state, (iii) a province-level test of whether designation follows annual haze exposure, and (iv) counts reported by four open early-warning prototypes that we built and self-audited across flood, haze and shelter data.

Of 20 environment and safety headline projects, 15 stop at sensing (including 3 security-surveillance projects), 2 name an alert or dispatch function, and none names a decision hub. Provinces with a certified city were not detectably more haze-exposed (median PM2.5 21.7 against 25.0 µg/m³; Cliff’s δ = −0.32, 95% CI −0.61 to +0.01; Holm p = 0.11) and published no more open data (median 298 against 299 datasets). In searches of one province, Chiang Mai, 41% of 2,869 resources could not be parsed, 6 of 311 datasets carried a location, none of 177 shelter rows (174 distinct shelters) had a coordinate, and no procurement dataset was found among 311 datasets and 682 catalogue resources.

The evidence is consistent with H3, weakly consistent with H1 and H4, and cannot test H2, because procurement itself is not observed. It is descriptive: it shows that gaps are present and measurable, not that they cause the stalled programmes, and it contains no outcome series. Four prototype systems built from public data show what could be bypassed (integration, speed, freshness labels) and what could not (authority, asset registers, the person at two in the morning). As an option worth testing, each certified smart city could publish one tested path from sensor to shelter, with a named duty officer.

Keywords smart city · digital government · Thailand · early warning · inter-agency coordination · public procurement · open data · monitoring-to-push chain

1Introduction

On 19 August 2026 the river in Nan province reached its banks about four hours after the rain began. The first reported disaster-agency cell broadcast followed about four hours after that; two people died and more than 16,000 were affected (Thiengburanathum & Arkaraprasertkul, 2026c). Every number needed to see the flood coming was already public. Thailand is not short of sensors, statutes or ranks: the United Nations places it 52nd of 193 in e-government in 2024, up from 55th in 2022, while its e-participation rank fell from 18th to 42nd over the same two years (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs [UN DESA], 2024). Smart-city and digital-government promotion has run since 2017 under the Digital Development for Economy and Society Act, the Digital Government Act and, for purchasing, the Procurement Act (Kingdom of Thailand, 2017a, 2017b, 2019). The label has spread. The chain from measurement to action has not closed.

Practitioners offer four explanations, and our research question states them as hypotheses to be checked, not conclusions. We treat each as a hypothesis with a stated observable implication and a stated falsifier (Table 1), and we report a verdict that is never stronger than the evidence. This paper is a synthesis. It adds three analyses of national records to the counts reported by four earlier prototype systems and self-audits that we built with the same four hypotheses in mind (Thiengburanathum & Arkaraprasertkul, 2026a–d). Two limits are stated here because they govern everything after. First, there is no outcome series: we cannot measure that promotion “failed”, only describe where the chain is open. Second, the second author works for the agency whose programme is studied, and both authors built the systems audited (Declarations).

2Background and hypotheses

Critics describe the smart city as an idea that sells faster than it ships: corporate storytelling (Söderström et al., 2014), the gap between rhetoric and the “actually existing” city (Shelton et al., 2015), and the question of which city is real (Hollands, 2008). A recurring finding is that technology is one of several dimensions, alongside people and institutions, and that governance is central (Nam & Pardo, 2011; Meijer & Rodríguez Bolívar, 2016). Thai case studies find a national pilot with no province-level implementation plan (Naprathansuk, 2017) and, in one province, progress in only some dimensions (Jumnong & Lowatcharin, 2022). A study of 36 cities identifies the core data sets that smart-city governance needs (Ruangwicha & Tuamsuk, 2026).

H1 Coordination. A hazard crosses agency boundaries that a budget does not. Joined-up government is easy to want and hard to build (Pollitt, 2003; Bardach, 1998), and collaboration needs stated conditions (Ansell & Gash, 2008); implementation decays with each agency whose consent it needs (Pressman & Wildavsky, 1973). H2 Procurement. Public IT projects often fail (Goldfinch, 2007; Heeks, 2006, for health information systems); demand-side procurement can drive innovation (Edler & Georghiou, 2007); and service-team models offer another way to buy (Mergel, 2019; Pahlka, 2023). H3 Responsiveness. A dashboard promises real-time governance (Kitchin, 2014; Kitchin et al., 2015), but open data has barriers and myths (Janssen et al., 2012), and, in our reading, a number becomes governance only when someone is obliged to act on it (cf. Mattern, 2015, on the urban dashboard). H4 End-to-end. Early warning is a chain of risk knowledge, detection and forecasting, communication, and response capability, and it is only as strong as its weakest link (Basher, 2006; World Meteorological Organization [WMO], 2018). The Sendai Framework and the Common Alerting Protocol make it a target and a format (United Nations General Assembly, 2015; OASIS, 2010). Thailand’s 2011 floods cost THB 1.43 trillion and affected more than 13 million people, and the World Bank recommended early warning built on real-time data (World Bank, 2012).

We combine the four into one model (Figure 1). Each hypothesis breaks a different part of a chain that runs sense, forecast, decide, push, shelter, verify. If the chain is the unit of analysis, a smart city is a set of working paths, and each path can be tested end to end.

3Data and methods

3.1 Evidence streams

E1, the national registry. The SCITI snapshot of 5 November 2026 (Arkaraprasertkul, 2026) holds 118 city records in 77 provinces, each with a status (certified, promotion zone, registered), an editorial reality class (operational, partial, planned, assigned by the SCITI team), and, for 49 cities, public-source metrics. depa’s registry lists 227 proposals as of January 2026 (220 existing-city and 7 new-city): 37 declared smart cities (data as of August 2025) and 190 promotion zones under review. SCITI holds 12 promotion-zone records, a subset of the 190. The government target is at least 105 smart cities for 2024–2027 (depa, 2024). E2, endorsed flagship records. For 21 of the 37 certified cities, depa’s pages publish an endorsed record with 3 to 6 key projects, one line each (about ten words): 76 headline projects in all (9 cities endorsed in 2021, 12 in 2022). Records of the same cities elsewhere list 28 to 60 projects, so the 76 are a census of the headline projects only, and how a project became a headline is unobserved. E3, province indicators. 20 open province indicators for up to 77 provinces (National Statistical Office, Pollution Control Department [PCD], Royal Forest Department, road-death integration system, Ministry of Energy, and the open-data portal), harvested 21 October 2026 (Arkaraprasertkul, 2026). E4, four prototype systems. Counts as reported by the four earlier preprints (Table 3): a national flood feed system, a national haze system, a Chiang Mai operations room with a shelter audit, and a Nakhon Si Thammarat control tower. We did not re-derive these counts; they are not independent of us, and several come from one-province searches (Chiang Mai, Nakhon Si Thammarat), which cannot show a national absence.

3.2 Coding and statistics

The second author coded each of the 76 headline projects from its one-line title on two fields (the coding table lives in the analysis script, which was drafted with AI assistance, so the codes are the second author’s judgements recorded in code): whether the stated domain is environmental monitoring, a natural hazard or public safety, and the highest link of the chain the text states (none, sense, decide, push; “programme only” if it names a programme rather than a function). Service platforms such as e-service, MaaS and tourism platforms are coded none, because they serve transactions, not the chain. A title is an endorsed vision, not evidence of delivery, and cannot name a path in detail; the H3 and H4 falsifiers therefore could hardly fail on this data type. Coding was by one coder, so the rules are published with the data, and Section 4.2 reports how sensitive the headline counts are to defensible re-coding. Proportions carry Wilson intervals (Wilson, 1927); they describe coding and counting uncertainty over a census of headline projects (or of one search), not sampling from a population.

For the province analysis a province is designated if it hosts at least one certified city (25 of 77). We compare designated and other provinces with the Mann–Whitney test (Mann & Whitney, 1947), report Cliff’s δ as the effect size (Cliff, 1993) with a percentile bootstrap interval (5,000 resamples, seed 20261105; Efron, 1979), and adjust four tests with Holm’s method (Holm, 1979). The four are not independent (the count and the per-100,000 rate share a variable), and provinces are treated as independent units although haze and designation both cluster by region. These tests are exploratory, and with 22 against 43 provinces only large differences are detectable. Population is the 2022 National Statistical Office ICT-survey base (65.6 million in total), used only to scale. A flood-frequency score exists for 30 provinces, but it is SCITI’s own 0-100 editorial rating, informed by GISTDA flood polygons (higher means less flood-prone), built for the livability pillar, and 25 of the 30 scored provinces host a certified city. Selection into the score follows certification, so there is no valid comparison group and we ran no flood comparison.

4Results

4.1 The programme funnel narrows at every step

Of 227 proposals on depa’s registry, 37 are declared (certified) (16.3%, 95% CI 12.1 to 21.7%), which is 35% of the target of 105 in a plan period that runs to 2027 (Figure 2); the funnel is not evidence of failure by itself, because many of the 190 proposals under review were submitted recently. The label tracks delivery imperfectly on the SCITI team’s own reality class: of the 37 certified cities, 30 are operational, 4 partial and 3 planned (8.1%, CI 2.8 to 21.3%). All 69 registered cities are planned. Of the 37 certified cities, 21 (57%) have a published flagship record; 16 of those 21 name at least one environment or safety headline project that senses or goes further (14 of 21 if the three security-surveillance projects are set aside); two name an alert or dispatch function.

4.2 Headline projects sense; they rarely decide or push

Of 76 headline projects, 20 (26%) sit in the environment, hazard or safety domain (Figure 3). Fifteen of the 20 stop at sensing (75%, CI 53 to 89%): river, watershed and coastal telemetry, air and water quality monitoring, and three security-surveillance projects (CCTV and crime monitoring in Phuket, Yala and Saensuk), which are not links in a hazard-warning chain. Two name a push function: a flood early-warning system in Hat Yai and digital ambulance dispatch in Khon Kaen. One names a “disaster-resilience programme” without a function, and two name none. The strict estimate of push is 2 of 20 (10%, CI 3 to 30%); counting the programme it is 3 of 20 (15%). Five projects across the whole set describe a decision hub (Phuket’s operation centre and data platform, the Andaman tourism twin in two records, a campus twin in Rayong), and none is described, in its title, as receiving environmental or safety data; this is silence in a ten-word title, not evidence of absence. Partner names appear for 8 of 9 records endorsed in 2021 and for none of 12 endorsed in 2022; this probably reflects what depa’s pages publish and supports no inference about coordination.

Sensitivity. The headline integers depend on judgement calls. Setting aside the three security-surveillance projects leaves 12 of 17 hazard-domain projects at sensing (71%) and 14 of 21 cities with a sensing project. Dropping two projects whose domain is arguable under our rule (a landscape-restoration corridor in Yala and island utilities in Samui) leaves 15 of 18 (83%). Doing both leaves 12 of 15 (80%). Counting Khon Kaen’s ambulance dispatch as decision support rather than a push leaves one push. In every variant sensing remains the majority, push is one or two, and no hazard-domain project reaches a decision hub.

4.3 Designation and haze exposure: no evidence of targeting

If promotion were steered by need, provinces with a certified city might show more exposure, and, as places expected to hold a data mandate, might publish more open data. We looked at annual haze only. Neither appears (Figure 4, Table 2). Provinces with a certified city had a median annual PM2.5 of 21.7 µg/m³ against 25.0 elsewhere; the direction is negative but the interval touches zero and the difference does not survive adjustment for four tests (δ = −0.32, CI −0.61 to +0.01; unadjusted p = 0.037, Holm p = 0.11), so this is no evidence of a difference rather than evidence of an opposite one. Region is a built-in confound: 11 of the 25 designated provinces are in the South (44%, against 14 of 77, 18%, of all provinces), where annual PM2.5 is low. Provincial values are means of station annual means, and stations are unevenly spread (Bangkok has many, most provinces one). Twelve provinces are absent from the PCD 2024 annual table, three of them certified (Krabi, Pattani, Phang Nga); PCD’s live network of 175 stations leaves only Phetchaburi, Phang Nga and Ranong uncovered (Thiengburanathum & Arkaraprasertkul, 2026b), so Phang Nga is the one certified province with no station. The median number of open datasets is the same, 298 against 299 (δ = −0.12, Holm p = 0.41). Per 100,000 residents designated provinces publish fewer (32 against 60; δ = −0.52, Holm p < 0.01), but this is a population effect, since count and rate share a variable: designated provinces are larger (median 852 against 506 thousand; δ = +0.43, unadjusted p = 0.003, a post hoc test) while their dataset counts are the same. What designation tracks in these data is size, not annual haze exposure.

4.4 Gap counts in the audited open data

The four earlier audits give counts across the four hypotheses (Figure 5). The Chiang Mai figures come from one province-keyed search, whose 311 datasets are what that search returned, not a census of hazard data, and a one-province absence cannot show a national one. On H1, only 6 of 311 Chiang Mai datasets (1.9%) carry a location; 79 of 175 national PM2.5 stations (45%) stand in Bangkok and three provinces have none; two official sources put a province in different air-quality bands on 23% of province-days, though 88% of those disagreements are between the two cleanest bands and an offset between sources largely explains them; and two agencies’ rain gauges within 2 km of each other correlated at r = 0.67 across 40 closest pairs and 738 hours, partly because a convective cell is real (Thiengburanathum & Arkaraprasertkul, 2026a–c). On H2, no procurement dataset appears among 311 datasets or 682 catalogue resources, 41% of 2,869 resources could not be parsed and 17% of datasets state no licence; this is an indirect indicator only. On H3, the median Chiang Mai dataset was 1.1 years old (the Nakhon Si Thammarat catalogue median is 236 days); 2,230 of 10,412 registry gauge records (21.4%, including non-Thai and global series, not a census of Thai gauges) were flagged stale; and 114 of 173 rain and 30 of 51 river records in one province had no observation in the previous two hours, a dated coverage measurement, not a verdict of outage. On H4, none of 177 official shelter rows (174 distinct) carries a coordinate (Wilson CI 0 to 2.1%), 73 of the 146 reporting toilets exceed 20 people per toilet at stated capacity (hypothetical full occupancy), and no drainage network, evacuation route or clean-air room dataset was found. Fresh counts exist too: 24 of 25 Chiang Mai feeds with a declared limit were inside it, 95.3% of PCD pulls were under two hours old, and 97.2% of flood-feed runs succeeded, so the picture is a partial, not a general, staleness. Sensor-layer faults also appear (only about half of 1,479 river gauges reported in the last three hours; 36 carry impossible bank levels), which cuts against placing the whole gap downstream of the sensors.

4.5 Four prototype systems: what could be bypassed

We built four prototype systems from public data without any inter-agency agreement (Table 3); none issues a sanctioned public alert. Integration, speed and freshness were bypassable: one system joined 44 flood feeds and raised alerts a median 6.7 minutes after a gauge reading (over 162 alerts that could be matched to a reading, excluding delivery to a phone); another read 16 haze feeds with 98.7% of 58,298 pulls logged as successful and handed an alert to Telegram test chats a median 7 seconds after it was raised, a hand-off and not arrival on a device; a third assembled gauges, fires, forecast and shelters within seven calendar weeks, adapting an earlier system. Reach, authority and assets were not. The flood system’s watchlist reached level 5 on 302 of 358 station-days that later did so, at a median lead of 8.2 hours and 12.5% precision, scored against the same gauge network the system reads (a coherence test, not accuracy, over 40 days), and it had 29 subscribers; the haze system’s forecast over-called on 10 of 13 checkable province-days; the Chiang Mai manual still lists its owner, operator and duty officer as to be assigned; and in Nakhon Si Thammarat two audit checks 145 minutes apart recorded a threshold exceedance with declaration status zero and then one declared watch, which establishes neither issuance nor delivery time.

5Discussion

The four gaps may look less like four failures than one. Each agency does its job and publishes the record of it, a table or a PDF, because that is what a project closes with; the open-data estate we sampled is the sum of those closings (Thiengburanathum & Arkaraprasertkul, 2026a). A record that ends a project does not move a person; a service that never ends might. One reading is that procurement of the first kind produces data of the first kind, and that coordination is hard because little live exists to coordinate around; but H2 is unobserved here, so this is a hypothesis, not a result. The registry shows the same shape in programme form: certification is a one-time event, and the endorsed titles describe sensors far more often than the people who answer at two in the morning. The reading is consistent with our data and with the literature on public IT (Goldfinch, 2007; Pahlka, 2023), not shown by it. It also fits “isomorphic mimicry”, forms without the capability behind them (Pritchett et al., 2013); our evidence cannot distinguish the two. Nor is the sensor layer blameless: only about half of the river gauges in one audit had reported in the previous three hours, and some carried impossible bank levels.

The province analysis adds a caution against assuming that promotion is steered by annual haze exposure: designation tracks larger provinces, we found no evidence that it tracks more exposed ones, and one certified province (Phang Nga) has no PM2.5 station. A label awarded for the quality of a proposal might follow the capacity to write proposals; whether that is why is untested here, and region, not size, may be doing the work.

Four acts follow from the gaps, offered as options to test, not as findings; none is costed, and only the first has been tried, by us, on a small scale. (1) Publish the three records a chain needs and the audited estate lacks: shelter coordinates, drainage geometry, and warning-tower locations with audible range. (2) Consider requiring, in each technology purchase, a documented machine interface, a licence and a freshness limit, so silence is visible; the literature supports demand-side procurement and service teams in general, not this specific rule (Edler & Georghiou, 2007; Mergel, 2019). (3) Carry every official warning in the Common Alerting Protocol (OASIS, 2010). (4) Ask each certified city to demonstrate one path from sensor to shelter, with a named duty officer and a scored warning log; none of our four prototypes has yet shown one end to end. The test is simple: could a stranger be moved by it?

6Limitations and threats to validity

  • Interests. The second author is a depa official and a builder of the SCITI index, of the SCITI reality classes, and of the four systems. The first author’s institution sponsors the case study. We report gaps in the second author’s own sector for that reason. A reader should weigh the SCITI classes and the flagship coding accordingly; no one outside the authors coded them.
  • Design. Cross-sectional 2026 data, no counterfactual, no outcome series, and four self-built systems whose audits we ran ourselves. The audits describe what a provincial or national user can reach; absence from a title-and-tag search of one province is not absence elsewhere. The 12 plotted counts follow a stated rule but were taken from the companions, which also report matching fresh counts.
  • Coding. One coder, who is conflicted, working from one-line titles of 3 to 6 headline projects per city that may understate or overstate delivery; a title cannot name a response rule or a path, so the H3 and H4 falsifiers could hardly fail. Only 21 of 37 certified cities have a published record; the other 16 are unobserved. A second independent coder and an agreement statistic are needed.
  • Provinces. Cities are nested in 77 provinces and values for cities are shared within a province, so we analysed provinces. Power is low (22 against 43), the PM2.5 value is a mean of station annual means that is missing in 12 provinces, provinces are not independent (haze and designation cluster by region), and 12 counts in Figure 5 were taken from the companion papers rather than recomputed. No flood comparison was possible.
  • Procurement. H2 is not observed, and rival explanations (funding, skills, central–local authority) are not excluded.

7Conclusion

In the national records and the four prototype systems we examined, the chain is open mostly where one agency’s record has to become another’s decision and a stranger’s message: in the searches we ran, records rarely join, much data is old or silent, and few headline projects name a path that reaches a shelter. The sensor layer has faults of its own. Procurement may belong on the list, but nobody has yet looked at it with data that can answer. We have shown that the gaps are present, measurable, and in part bridgeable by open means; that the bridge stops at authority and asset registers; and that we found no evidence that designation tracks annual haze exposure. Whether the gaps explain the stalled programmes remains to be tested with procurement records, interviews and outcome series. We offer the frozen data, the coding and a single script so that someone outside our offices can try.
